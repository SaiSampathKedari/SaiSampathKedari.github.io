When More Data Cannot Separate Two Parameters

Identifiability in an SIR epidemic model: why the Fisher information for (γ, κ) has rank one, what a Laplace approximation and an adaptive sampler each report, and what is still open.

Course project 2024 (AEROSP 567), rebuilt 2025 · Code

The model

An SIR model tracks susceptible, infected, and recovered counts in a population of $N = 1000$:

$$\dot S = \delta N - \delta S - \gamma\kappa\, S I,\qquad \dot I = \gamma\kappa\, S I - (r+\delta) I,\qquad \dot R = r I - \delta R.$$

We observe only the infected count $I(t)$, with Gaussian noise of standard deviation 50, and want the posterior over $\theta = (\gamma, \kappa, r, \delta)$ under a standard normal prior. The data were generated with $\beta = \gamma\kappa = 0.02$, $r = 0.6$, and $\delta = 0.15$. The pair $\gamma = 0.01$, $\kappa = 2$ serves as a reference point on the curve $\gamma\kappa = 0.02$.

Why the data cannot separate $\gamma$ and $\kappa$

The two parameters enter the dynamics only through their product $\beta = \gamma\kappa$. Any pair with the same product produces exactly the same trajectory, so the likelihood is constant along each curve $\gamma\kappa = c$. More data sharpens the estimate of $\beta$, but it cannot say how $\beta$ splits into its two factors.

The Fisher information makes this precise. By the chain rule, with $I_\beta$ the information about $\beta$,

$$I(\gamma,\kappa) = I_\beta \begin{bmatrix}\kappa \\ \gamma\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\kappa & \gamma\end{bmatrix}, \qquad I(\gamma,\kappa)\begin{bmatrix}\gamma \\ -\kappa\end{bmatrix} = 0.$$

This block has rank one. Moving in the direction $(\gamma, -\kappa)$ leaves the likelihood unchanged to first order, and that direction is tangent to the curve $\gamma\kappa = c$. The full $4 \times 4$ matrix, which also covers $r$ and $\delta$, has rank three, with null vector $(\gamma, -\kappa, 0, 0)$.

What the two methods report

Laplace approximation. It fits a single Gaussian at the posterior mode it finds, here $\gamma = \kappa \approx 0.137$. The fitted covariance has a correlation of almost exactly $-1$ between $\gamma$ and $\kappa$, so the Gaussian is a straight line tangent to the curved ridge. Its 95% interval for $\kappa$ runs from about $-0.86$ to $1.13$, but the line leaves the curve almost at once: a step of $0.1$ along it from the mode already halves $\gamma\kappa$, so most of its mass sits where the data fit badly. Its width is not even stable: across five draws of the observation noise, the reported variance of $\kappa$ ranged from $0.0003$ to $0.25$, because the curvature along the ridge is zero and the optimizer's estimate of it reflects its own path.

DRAM sampling. Delayed-rejection adaptive Metropolis follows the ridge and recovers its curved shape, but only on one branch. Because the likelihood depends only on $\gamma\kappa$ and the standard normal prior is symmetric, the mirror-image branch, where both parameters are negative, carries exactly the same posterior mass. It exists only because the prior allows negative rates. Moving between the branches means passing near $\gamma\kappa = 0$, where the data fit badly, so the chain never crosses and reports half of the posterior. Mixing is also slow along the branch: after burn-in, the chain moves on about 4.5% of iterations.

Neither method reaches the reference value $\kappa = 2$. The likelihood is flat along the curve, so the prior decides where along the curve the posterior mass sits, and under the standard normal prior $\kappa = 2$ is unlikely.

What remains open

Sampling the ridge. Reparameterizing in terms of $\beta = \gamma\kappa$ and one coordinate along the curve should make each branch easier to sample. Moving between the two branches needs more than local proposals, for example a move that uses the symmetry $(\gamma, \kappa) \to (-\gamma, -\kappa)$, or tempering. Comparing these with samplers that adapt to curved geometry is the next step.

Prediction versus intervention. Because every pair on the curve produces the same trajectory, the posterior forecasts the epidemic well. It also answers proportional changes: halving $\gamma$ halves $\beta$, which the data determine. It cannot answer what happens if $\gamma$ is set to a new value, because the resulting rate then depends on $\kappa$, which the data never pinned down.
